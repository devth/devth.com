# Keep the world small

Small teams.
Small infra.
Small codebases.
Small vendor list.
Small dependencies.

## How do you do this?

Create boundaries between product areas.
Imagine if you needed to build Stripe and you couldn't just use Stripe.

## Developers are not fungible

It's a nice thought though.

When 1 or 2 or 3 engineers fully own a system for years, they have incredibly
deep knowledge of it. That knowledge can't be gained quickly. It literally
happens over those years. So when you hire someone and allocate them to a team,
you're making a high-risk potentially very-expensive bet that they will
eventually gain the knowledge to be as productive as the engineer who's already
worked on it for 3 years. And if you re-org, move people around, and have a
general lack of stability where leadership is constantly churned, you'll never
allow that engineer to reach their potential productivity or anywhere near it.
We all talk about the mythical 10x engineer, but what you're doing instead is 
creating an environment that caps engineers at 0.10x productivity.


## The problem with engineering leadership

Most engineering leaders are too immature and inexperienced to understand this.
They are people who present well, continually posture, and curate an image
around sounding intellectual rather than building and honing and practicing the
hard, fragile, atrophy-prone skill of software engineering.

And their bosses don't know any different, because they got to where they are by
doing the same thing.

Every now and then you get a leader who is (or was recently) a builder, and they
manage 100x better than their peers because they get it, but too often they are
forced out by politics and high-school-level drama.
